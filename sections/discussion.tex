\paragraph{Rethinking the safety calibration target.}
All evaluated mechanisms are calibrated on binary distinctions, concentrating protection at L3 while leaving the L1--L2 regime underserved; defenses trained this way steepen the severity gradient rather than closing it, so deployments in sensitive contexts must evaluate the L1--L2 regime explicitly rather than trusting L3-derived safety claims. Severity-aware defense (optimizing for L1 and L2 suppression using ASL-generated training data) is the most direct path to closing the gap.

\paragraph{The cross-modal gap as a root cause.}
Text filters cannot decode visually adversarial prompts, and image classifiers trained on explicit L3 content fail at L1--L2 violations: the \red{72.32\%} stealthy attack rate at L3 shows this gap is by adversarial design. Future systems require classifiers trained jointly on text and image signals across the full severity spectrum.

\paragraph{Category-specific implications.}
Copyright and PID exhibit structurally lower HGR than NSFW categories (\Tab{\ref{tab:t2i_models}}), not because models are safer there but because MLLM classifiers are weaker at fine-grained identity harms (e.g., character likeness); the safety gap in these categories is likely \emph{underestimated}.

\red{\paragraph{Cross-cultural scope.}
Severity definitions reflect a Western (US/EU) legal framework inherited from current models' safety-alignment data. Our cultural nuance analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:cultural}) shows non-Western entities are less protected ($13.4$ pp higher L3 HGR for Public Figures and $11.2$ pp for Copyright), pointing to underrepresentation in safety fine-tuning data. We treat this as an explicit scope limitation and a foundation for a multilingual, multi-jurisdictional extension of \dataname. We will also add finer severity gradations at drastic L2$\to$L3 transitions (e.g., ``nudity behind objects'' to pornography) in the next public release.}
