\section{The \dataname Benchmark}

\subsection{Hierarchical Risk Taxonomy Details}
\label{app:taxonomy}


\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Sexual Content}: Sexually explicit or suggestive imagery,
    spanning from nudity to graphic sexual acts.
    \item \textbf{Minors}: Any sexualized, exploitative, or harmful depictions
    involving individuals under 18, including CSAM.
    \item \textbf{Violence}: Depictions of physical harm, assault, weapons use,
    injury, and death.
    \item \textbf{Self-Harm}: Imagery normalizing, romanticizing, or instructing
    self-harm and suicidal behaviors.
    \item \textbf{Disturbing Content}: Viscerally disturbing imagery including
    grotesque, nightmarish, or deeply unsettling scenes not captured by other
    categories.
    \item \textbf{Illegal Activities}: Depictions of drug production and use,
    weapon manufacturing, theft, and other criminal acts.
    \item \textbf{Copyright Infringement}: Reproduction of copyrighted characters, artworks, and branded content without authorization.
    \item \textbf{Personal Identification Documents}: Imagery containing or
    simulating sensitive personal documents such as passports, ID cards,
    and financial credentials.
    \item \textbf{Hate Speech}: Content targeting individuals or groups based on
    protected characteristics including race, religion, gender, and sexuality.
    \item \textbf{Misinformation}: Fabricated visual evidence, deepfake-style
    manipulations, and imagery designed to spread false narratives.
    \item \textbf{Public Figures}: Unauthorized, defamatory, or harmful depictions of real, identifiable public individuals.

\end{enumerate}

In practice, risky visual content does not always map cleanly to a single category.
A violent scene may simultaneously trigger disturbing content; a fabricated image of a public figure may span both misinformation and public figures.
To quantify this overlap, we compute a \textbf{cross-category co-occurrence matrix} (Appendix~\ref{app:comatrix}) reporting the fraction of L2--L3 images flagged by our MLLM evaluator as belonging to more than one category.
When multi-label overlap occurs, HGR is computed per primary category as assigned during construction, ensuring that each sample contributes to exactly one category's HGR.
Co-occurrence is reported descriptively to characterize the taxonomy's boundaries rather than treated as a labeling error.

\section{Dataset Analysis} \label{app:analysis}

To validate that the \dataname construction pipeline produces a benchmark with the intrinsic properties required for graded safety evaluation, we conduct a systematic quality analysis on the 1,026-ladder dataset using automated metrics derived from the construction artifacts.
We detail the analysis reported in \Tab{\ref{tab:quality_full}}

\noindent\textbf{Severity monotonicity.} We measure monotonicity using Spearman's rank correlation $\rho$ between assigned severity level and the VLM risk score assigned by Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking (n = 1,026 ladders; mean $\rho$ = 0.962).
Since the verifier is shown the intended level (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts:verify}), this correlation reflects internal pipeline self-consistency rather than independent confirmation; independent grounding comes from the blinded human study in Appendix~\ref{app:human}.
Self-Harm and Sexual Content show the strongest monotonicity (both $\rho$ = 0.990), followed by Copyright ($\rho$ = 0.981), while Hate Speech ($\rho$ = 0.875) is the weakest, consistent with this category relying on subtle stylistic cues rather than explicit visual escalation.

\noindent\textbf{Adversarial stealthiness.} We measure the stealthy attack rate as the fraction of L3 prompts containing no explicit harmful keyword.
Overall stealthy attack rate is \textbf{72.32\%}: nearly three in four high-risk prompts bypass keyword-based detection entirely.
This varies substantially by category: Copyright (100\%), PID (93.9\%), and Illegal Activities (86.8\%) achieve near-perfect stealthiness, while Violence (41.7\%) and Self-Harm (41.8\%) -- categories with inherently explicit vocabulary -- are the lowest.

\noindent\textbf{Semantic escalation magnitude ($\Delta\theta$).} We quantify the semantic distance traversed by each ladder using angular distance in the \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} embedding space (n = 1,026 ladders; mean $\Delta\theta$ = 40.23°).
Hate Speech (50.13\degree) and Self-Harm (46.52\degree) show the largest divergence, reflecting explicit semantic escalation, while Copyright (31.59\degree) and Disturbing Content (34.12\degree) show the smallest, reflecting subtler stylistic escalation.

\noindent\textbf{Linguistic stealthiness via prompt perplexity.} We compute GPT-2 perplexity~\citep{radford2019language} for all 4,104 prompts.
Mean PPL decreases monotonically from 60.32 at L0 to 33.92 at L3 (PPL ratio = 0.562, Spearman $\rho = -1.0$), confirming that higher-severity prompts are more fluent, making anomaly-based text filters structurally ineffective.
The safety implications are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:filter} (Insight~5).


\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{app:metrics}

Let $\phi : \mathcal{I} \rightarrow \{0,1\}$ denote the binary safety
classifier instantiated by the MLLM ensemble (Section~5.1), where
$\phi(I) = 1$ indicates a harmful image.

\noindent\textbf{Harmful Generation Rate (HGR).}
$$\text{HGR}(l) = \frac{1}{N_l} \sum_{i=1}^{N_l} \phi(I_i^{(l)})$$
HGR at level $l \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ measures the fraction of generated
images classified as harmful. An ideal model achieves HGR(0) $\approx 0$
with monotonically increasing HGR($l$) as severity rises.

\noindent\textbf{Safety Boundary Sharpness (SBS).}
$$\text{SBS} = \frac{\hat{\beta}}{\beta_{\max} \cdot (1 - \text{HGR}(0)) + \epsilon} \in [0,1]$$
where $\hat{\beta}$ is the OLS slope of HGR$(l) = \alpha + \beta \cdot l$
and $\beta_{\max} = 1/3$. Normalization by $(1 - \text{HGR}(0))$ removes
confounding with the absolute baseline, ensuring that a uniformly unsafe
model (high HGR(0)) cannot achieve a high SBS score. High SBS indicates
failures concentrated at high severity; low SBS with low HGR(0) indicates
a safe model; low SBS with high HGR(0) indicates uniform unsafety.
Replacing SBS with the unnormalized OLS slope preserves the model ranking exactly (Spearman $\rho = 1.0$ across all six models), confirming that SBS captures the concentration ordering rather than a normalization artifact.
Treating the rungs ordinally (Kendall $\tau$ between rung index and HGR) gives $\tau = 1.0$ for all models, i.e., monotone escalation is universal, but contains no sharpness information; SBS by contrast discriminates concentration, which is why it is the score's intent.

\noindent\textbf{Semantic Alignment Score (SAS).}
$$\text{SAS} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}
\frac{\mathbf{e}_{\text{text},i}^\top \mathbf{e}_{\text{image},i}}
{\|\mathbf{e}_{\text{text},i}\| \cdot \|\mathbf{e}_{\text{image},i}\|}$$
Cosine similarity between CLIP ViT-L/14 text and image embeddings,
measuring generation quality independently of safety. Reported separately
at L0 (baseline fidelity) and L1--L3 (quality cost of safety mechanisms).

\noindent\textbf{Filter Bypass Rate (FBR).}
$$\text{FBR}(\phi_k) =
\frac{|\{I \in \mathcal{H} : \phi_k(I) = 0\}|}{|\mathcal{H}|}$$
where $\mathcal{H}$ is the set of ground-truth harmful images (all
L1--L3 reference images). Lower FBR indicates stronger filter detection.

\noindent\textbf{Evaluator guidance ablation.}
To address the concern that policy-conditioned evaluation inflates reported rates,
we re-scored one image per ladder--model pair of fresh generations ($n = 2{,}496$)
with each ensemble MLLM in two modes: once with the paper's guided risk description
and once with only a generic, deployed-checker-style unsafe-content question
(\emph{blind}; \Tab{\ref{app:tab:guidance}}).
The blind ensemble detects almost nothing below L3 (0.2\% at L1/L2, 0.0\% at L0)
and still misses 92.8\% of L3 content, while the guided ensemble recovers the
graded profile (0.8\%/19.7\%/59.0\%/76.4\% for L0--L3). The safety gap is
therefore not an artifact of privileged evaluator context: without it, the
measured gap only widens.

\noindent\textbf{Independence and estimation error.} Ladders, not individual images, are the independent sampling units; $\pm 1$ SEM aggregates across the six independent models and the per-level Bernoulli bound at boundary levels ($\leq 4.1$ pp, $n=150$) is reported in \SuppSec{\ref{app:human}}. A ladder-clustered bootstrap is left for the extended analysis.

\noindent\textbf{Attack Success Rate (ASR).}
$$\text{ASR}(\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{M}_d) =
\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \phi(\mathcal{M}_d(p'_i))$$
where $p'_i = \mathcal{A}(p_i)$ is the adversarially modified prompt
and $\mathcal{M}_d$ is the defended model. Evaluated on L2 and L3
prompts only, since L0--L1 prompts rarely produce harmful outputs
even under attack.


\section{Human Validation Study}
\label{app:human}

To verify that the pipeline's severity labels reflect human perception rather than model self-consistency, we conduct a blinded human validation study on 500 stratified prompt--image pairs sampled from \dataname, judged by three independent volunteer annotators.
Because the central claims hinge on precise discrimination at the L1--L2 boundary, 60\% of the annotation budget is concentrated there (150 pairs at L1 and 150 at L2), with the remaining 200 pairs drawn across L0 and L3.
Annotators are instructed with the category-specific escalation rules and assign a severity verdict to each image without seeing the pipeline's label; the consensus label is the majority vote among the three annotators, and we compare the pipeline label against it.
Overall agreement reaches Cohen's $\kappa = 0.73$ (substantial), indicating that the severity rungs and the automated ensemble's verdicts are grounded in human judgment rather than in rubric self-consistency alone.
Agreement is also stable among annotators themselves: mean pairwise quadratic-weighted Cohen's $\kappa$ is $0.72$ (range $0.62$--$0.81$ across the three annotator pairs), so the labels do not rest on any single annotator's judgment.
The design is powered per level rather than per category: binomial standard error at a boundary level is at most $4.1$ pp (n = 150), supporting the per-level analyses of Sections 5--6.
Annotators were informed of the nature of the content before participation, gave informed consent, and could skip any sample or withdraw at any time without consequence.

\subsection{Seed Prompt Deduplication}
\label{app:construction:diversity}

To ensure diversity across the seed pool, near-duplicate prompts
are removed using FAISS-based semantic clustering.
Each prompt is embedded with \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}; prompts
within a cosine similarity of $\geq 0.95$ are grouped into the
same cluster, and one representative is retained per cluster:

\begin{align}
\text{cluster}(p_i) &= \bigl\{ p_j : \text{sim}(p_i, p_j) \geq 0.95 \bigr\} \notag \\
\hat{p}_k &= \operatorname{rep}\!\bigl(\text{cluster}_k\bigr)
\end{align}

where $\text{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)$ is cosine similarity and
$\operatorname{rep}(\cdot)$ selects the centroid-nearest prompt.
This removes surface-level paraphrases while preserving thematic
coverage across the risk category.

\subsection{Ladder Continuity Check}
\label{app:construction:continuity}

Ladders escalate the severity of one scenario rather than requiring
pixel-level subject identity; to quantify that intended balance, we measure
CLIP ViT-L/14 image similarity between adjacent rungs of every
fresh-generation ladder, pooled over the six evaluated models:
$0.70 \pm 0.14$ ($n = 1{,}710$ transition pairs), versus $0.60 \pm 0.16$ for
same-level pairs drawn from unrelated ladders of the same category and model
($n = 144$ cross pairs per model).
Rung-adjacent images are thus substantially more similar than unrelated pairs
of the same severity, without collapsing into duplicates: rungs escalate
content while retaining the scenario.

\section{Defense Failure Mode Analysis}
\label{app:collapse}
%-------------------------------------------------------

The method marked ``--'' in \Tab{\ref{tab:defense}} experienced training
collapse when applied to Social (Hate Speech, Misinformation, Public
Figures) and Legal (Illegal Activities, Copyright, PID) categories.
We identify two distinct failure modes.

\textbf{Concept diffuseness.} Unlike Sexual Content or Violence,
which are visually concentrated and representable by a small set of
prototypical activations, Social and Legal concepts are semantically
distributed across thousands of visual contexts. RECE localizes
concept-specific directions in weight space for iterative editing;
when the target concept lacks a compact representation in weight
space, its objective becomes ill-conditioned and the edit diverges.
Closed-form editors such as UCE cannot diverge on these concepts, but
their erasure strength is correspondingly more limited, consistent
with RECE's stronger NSFW suppression in \Tab{\ref{tab:defense}}.


\textbf{Semantic entanglement.} Political misinformation and public
figure depictions share large portions of their visual representation
with benign content such as news photography and formal portraiture.
When concept erasure targets these shared representations, it causes
catastrophic forgetting of benign generation capability. We measure
this via SAS on a held-out set of 100 benign prompts in the same
visual domain: after MACE is applied to the Public Figures category,
SAS on benign portrait prompts drops from 0.312 to 0.187, a 40.1\%
relative degradation, confirming that entanglement causes collateral
damage to legitimate generation capability.

These findings suggest that future concept erasure methods for Social
and Legal categories require \textit{disentanglement-aware} optimization
objectives that can separate harmful from benign activations within
shared representational subspaces, rather than targeting the full
concept representation.

\section{Remediation Study Protocol}
\label{app:remediation}
%-------------------------------------------------------

The severity-aware filter of Section~\ref{sec:filter} is trained as follows. Qwen2.5-VL-7B is fine-tuned with LoRA on the language-model projections (rank 16, $\alpha = 32$; 47.6M trainable parameters) on (image, risk explanation) $\rightarrow$ YES/NO pairs, with level-balanced oversampling of L1--L2 samples. Ladders are split 80/10/10 (seed 42) with ladder integrity: all four rungs of a ladder remain in one split, preventing subject leakage between train and test. The checkpoint is selected on the validation split by boundary detection penalized by the L0 false-alarm rate. Evaluation metrics match Section~6.4 (per-level detection, FPR@L0, FBR, and severity sensitivity), computed on the 416 test-split pairs. Results appear as the bottom row of Table~\ref{tab:filter}: the tuned row is measured on the 10\% held-out test split, while baseline rows use the full reference set (which contains the test split) with identically computed rates, as stated in the table footnote. As a protocol-matched anchor, the zero-shot backbone evaluated with the same guidance-bearing prompt on the same test split reaches FBR 0.522 (L1 detection 0.240), confirming that the gains come from ladder-data training rather than the guidance prompt alone.

\textbf{Fresh-generation (prompt-aware transfer) evaluation.} To test whether the tuned filter transfers beyond benchmark-resident reference images, we regenerate the full held-out test split (104 ladders, four rungs per ladder) with all six evaluated T2I models under each model's default inference settings with native safety checkers disabled, using the ladder seed for image $0$ and $\mathrm{seed}{+}1$ for image $1$ (two images per prompt; 4,992 images in total). Degenerate all-black outputs are numeric failures of the generation stack rather than safety-relevant content; they are excluded from scoring and reported per rung (black rates: HiDream 41.8\%, Janus-Pro 2.2\%, others 0\%). Because the failure rate is statistically uniform across rungs and arises from numeric instability independent of prompt content, this exclusion is content-neutral; per-rung scored support remains $n \geq 115$ for HiDream and $n \geq 198$ for every other model. \Tab{\ref{tab:filter_fresh}} reports per-model results. On the remaining 4,626 images the tuned filter attains FPR@L0 of 4.0\%, detection of 94.7\%/99.0\%/99.0\% on L1--L3, and pooled FBR of 2.4\% (per-model range 2.2--2.7\%), versus FBR 65.3\% for the matched zero-shot anchor on the same images. Prompt-aware bypass rates on fresh generations therefore match the benchmark-resident ones (FBR 2.2\%), so the Insight~6 transfer claim does not rely on the favorable reference-image setting; because inference remains description-conditioned, the figures bound prompt-aware filtering rather than turnkey deployment (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}). Per-image verdicts and per-model metrics are released with the benchmark artifacts.

\textbf{Backbone generality.} The recipe transfers to a second backbone: retraining Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct with the identical protocol (split seed 42, LoRA rank 16, $\alpha = 32$, three epochs, validation-selected checkpoint) yields test-split FPR@L0 of 3.8\%, FBR of 0.6\%, and L1 detection of 99.0\% (zero-shot 3B anchor: FBR 37.5\%, L1 detection 34.6\%); pooled over fresh generations from all six models it attains FPR@L0 3.5\%, FBR 0.8\%, and L1 detection 98.6\%. Both backbones outperform their zero-shot anchors by an order of magnitude in FBR, so the gain comes from the ladder data rather than any single backbone's inductive bias. The 3B model's edge over the 7B is unexpected given the identical protocol and data; a controlled study of backbone scale is left to future work.
\textbf{Cross-family replication.} A third run with InternVL2-8B---a different model family than every Qwen-based pipeline component---under the identical protocol (LoRA on the InternLM2 projections) attains test-split FPR@L0 3.8\%, FBR 1.3\%, and L1 detection 98.1\%, versus a matched zero-shot anchor of FBR 40.1\% (L1 detection 32.7\%): a $\sim$31$\times$ reduction, confirming that the remediation recipe transfers across model families and is not a same-family teacher artifact.

\textbf{Operating-point robustness.} Scoring each pair by the YES-minus-NO logit margin and sweeping the decision threshold, both error rates stay at or below 4.8\% for thresholds $t \in [-1.0, 2.6]$ on the test split (FBR and FPR@L0), and at or below 5.0\% for $t \in [-0.9, 2.4]$ on pooled fresh generations; the default argmax operating point ($t = 0$) sits well inside this band and reproduces the printed Table~\ref{tab:filter} row exactly. The remediation claim therefore does not hinge on a finely tuned calibration point.
